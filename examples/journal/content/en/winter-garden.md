From the kitchen window in January the garden looks finished, in the way a stage looks finished
after the play: a few props left out, everything the same grey. I used to stay inside until March
and feel vaguely guilty about it. Then a neighbour handed me a trowel and said the most useful thing
anyone has told me about gardens: *winter is when you can see the bones.*

## What the bare months show

With the leaves gone you can see the shape of things. Which branch of the old pear crosses another
and rubs it raw in the wind. Where water sits after rain. Which corner gets the low sun at noon and
which never sees it at all[^shade]. In June all of that hides under green, and you plant by habit
instead of by looking.

So I walk the garden once a week with a notebook and write down only what I can see:

- the puddle by the shed, again, two days after rain;
- frost staying all day along the north fence;
- blackbirds turning over the leaf pile, which means there is something in it worth eating;
- the first garlic shoots, pale and very sure of themselves.

## Small jobs for short days

Winter jobs are the kind you can stop halfway through when your fingers go numb. Cut back the
dead stems you left standing for the insects, but only once the weather turns mild. Sharpen the
secateurs. Clean pots. Sow sweet peas on a cold windowsill, where they grow slowly and stockily,
which is exactly what you want from them.

> A garden is never as empty as it looks. It is only quiet.

## Leaving some mess

The tidiest gardens are the worst places to spend a winter if you are a beetle. A pile of logs, a
corner of uncut grass and a few hollow stems give a surprising amount of life somewhere to sleep.
I leave one bed untouched until April now. It looks untidy from the window, and that is the point.

[^shade]: A cheap compass and a notebook beat any app here: note the time when each bed goes into shade, once a month.
