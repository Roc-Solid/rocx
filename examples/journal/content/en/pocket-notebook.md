I have carried a notebook the size of a playing card for eleven years. It lives in whatever coat
I am wearing, with a short pencil tucked into the elastic. People sometimes ask why I don't simply
use my phone, which is in the other pocket. It's a fair question, and the answer turns out to be
about friction.

## Writing it down is a decision

Opening a phone to note something means passing through a lock screen, a wall of notifications
and at least one app that would like a moment of your time. By the time the note app is open, the
thought is often gone, or it has been replaced by somebody else's. A notebook opens to a blank
page in one movement. Nothing in it wants anything from you.

There is a second, quieter effect. Because space is limited, I write less and choose more. A page
of a pocket notebook holds about forty words, so I write the forty that matter[^pages].

## A small system

Over the years the notebook has collected a few habits, and none of them are clever:

1. Every page gets a date in the top corner, nothing else.
2. A dash is a task, a circle is an appointment, a plain line is a thought.
3. When a task is done, it gets a tick. When it's moved, an arrow.
4. On Sunday evening I read the week's pages and copy anything still alive to a fresh page.

That last step does most of the work. Copying a task by hand is just tedious enough that I only
copy what I still care about. The rest quietly dies on the old page, which is where it belongs.

## What the notebooks became

I keep the filled ones in a shoebox. I rarely look things up in them, but when I do, I find more
than I came for: a phone number next to a sketch of a bridge, a shopping list, half a poem, the
name of a song somebody played in a café in 2019[^songs]. A searchable archive would be more useful,
I suppose. It would also be much less interesting to read.

[^pages]: Mine have 64 pages, which last about five weeks. A cheaper notebook lasts just as long.
[^songs]: The song was very good. I still haven't found out who played it.
