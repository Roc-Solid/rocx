The bread I make most often takes about forty-five hours from the first spoonful of flour to the
first slice. That sounds like a hobby for people with no jobs. It isn't. Of those forty-five hours,
I work for perhaps twenty minutes. The rest of the time the dough works and I go to bed.

## Time does the kneading

Flour and water left together quietly form gluten on their own. A wet dough that rests overnight
needs almost no kneading: a few folds when you pass it in the kitchen are plenty. A cool fridge
slows the yeast down so that flavour has time to develop before the dough runs out of food[^cold].

## The numbers I actually use

Bakers write recipes as percentages of the flour's weight, which makes them easy to scale. This is
my everyday loaf:

| Ingredient     | Baker's % | For one loaf |
|:---------------|----------:|-------------:|
| Bread flour    |      90 % |        450 g |
| Wholemeal rye  |      10 % |         50 g |
| Water          |      72 % |        360 g |
| Salt           |       2 % |         10 g |
| Ripe starter   |      15 % |         75 g |

And this is the timetable, more or less:

| When              | What happens                         | Your work |
|:------------------|:-------------------------------------|:---------:|
| Friday, 9 pm      | Mix everything, cover, leave out     |   5 min   |
| Saturday, 8 am    | Three folds, an hour apart           |   3 min   |
| Saturday, noon    | Shape, into a basket, into the fridge |   5 min   |
| Sunday, 6 pm      | Bake straight from the fridge        |   5 min   |

## What goes wrong

Almost every bad loaf I have made was rushed, not neglected. A dough that seems slow is usually
just cold. Give it another hour somewhere warm before deciding it has failed. A loaf that spreads
flat in the oven was shaped too loosely, or proved too long at room temperature, not in the fridge.

> The recipe is mostly patience, written down as a timetable.

## Baking it

Heat the oven with a lidded pot inside for a good forty minutes. Tip the dough onto a square of
baking paper, cut it once with a sharp blade, and lower it into the pot. Twenty minutes with the
lid on, twenty-five with it off, until the crust is darker than you think it should be. Then the
hardest part: wait an hour before cutting it[^wait].

[^cold]: Around 4 °C the yeast nearly stops, but the bacteria in a sourdough starter keep making the acids that give the bread its taste.
[^wait]: The inside is still cooking as it cools. Cut too early and the crumb turns gummy.
