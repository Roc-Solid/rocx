Off the coast where I spend my summers there is a small island you can walk to at low tide. Every
year somebody walks out in the afternoon sun, sits down with a book, and has to be fetched by boat
in the evening. The tide table pinned outside the café would have saved them. It looks like a
spreadsheet, but it is easy to read once you know what you are looking at.

## What the table says

A table for one day usually lists four times: two high waters and two low waters, with the height
of the water at each, measured from a fixed level on the charts[^datum].

| Tide | Time  | Height |
|:-----|:-----:|-------:|
| Low  | 04:12 |  0.6 m |
| High | 10:31 |  4.9 m |
| Low  | 16:40 |  0.8 m |
| High | 22:55 |  4.7 m |

The gap between a high and the next low is a little over six hours, and every day the whole
pattern arrives about fifty minutes later than the day before.

![A curve of the water's height over a day, rising to two highs and falling to two lows.](/images/tide.svg "One day of tides: two highs and two lows, a little over six hours apart.")

## The rule of twelfths

The water doesn't rise at a steady pace. It starts slowly, hurries in the middle and slows down
again near the top. Sailors estimate it with the rule of twelfths: in each of the six hours from
low to high, the water rises by

- one twelfth of the range in the first hour,
- two twelfths in the second,
- three in the third and three in the fourth,
- two in the fifth,
- and one in the last.

So halfway between low and high water, the sea has already covered half of its range, and during
the two middle hours it comes in fastest. That is exactly when people get caught on the causeway.

## Planning the walk

If low water is at 16:40, set out an hour before it and start back no later than an hour after.
That gives you two hours on the island when the water is near its lowest, and a comfortable
margin. Spring tides, around the full and new moon, go out further and come in faster; neap tides
in between are gentler. The café's table marks both, and it is worth reading the small print.

[^datum]: That level is roughly the lowest the tide ever falls, which is why heights in the table are almost never negative.
