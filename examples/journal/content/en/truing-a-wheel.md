A wheel wobbles because some of its spokes are tighter than others. That's all. The rim is held
in shape by thirty-odd thin wires pulling against each other, and when one loosens, the rim drifts
towards the side that pulls harder. Truing is the slow business of rebalancing that pull, a quarter
turn at a time.

![A bicycle wheel seen from the side, its spokes crossing in pairs towards the hub.](/images/wheel.svg "Spokes alternate between the hub's two sides: tightening one pulls the rim its way.")

## What you need

- a spoke key that fits your nipples snugly (a loose one rounds them off);
- a cable tie, or a pencil taped to the frame, as a pointer;
- a bowl for small parts, because there are always small parts;
- an hour without interruptions.

You don't need a truing stand. Turn the bicycle upside down, or hang it from a hook, and use the
frame itself.

## The method

1. Spin the wheel slowly and watch the gap between the rim and the brake pad, or your cable-tie
   pointer, to find where it rubs.
2. Mark the middle of the wobble with a piece of tape.
3. At the tape, find the spokes that come from the side opposite the wobble. Tighten each by a
   quarter turn.
4. Loosen the spokes from the same side as the wobble by a quarter turn, so the wheel stays
   evenly tensioned.
5. Spin again. Repeat with smaller and smaller turns until the rim passes the pointer with a
   steady gap.

The spoke key turns the opposite way to what you expect, because you are looking at the nipple
from above, not from the hub. Everyone gets this wrong the first time. Most people get it wrong
the second time as well.

## Knowing when to stop

A wheel that wobbles by less than a millimetre is a wheel that works. Chasing perfection on a
kitchen table usually means over-tightening something, and an over-tight spoke is more likely to
snap on the road than a slightly loose one. Squeeze pairs of spokes together around the wheel when
you finish; if they all feel about the same, you are done.
