The ticket said departure 21:47, platform 9, carriage 214, berth 63. I arrived forty minutes early,
which is a habit night trains encourage, and bought bread, cheese and two apples from the kiosk.
By the time the train came in, sliding along the platform with its blinds already half down, it
felt less like boarding transport and more like checking into a very narrow hotel.

## The compartment

A couchette compartment holds six bunks, three on each side, folded against the walls by day. Mine
had a small window, a reading light that worked and one that didn't, a ladder, a hook for a
jacket and a bottle of water on each pillow. The other passengers arrived one by one: a student
with a cello case she slept beside like a companion, an older man with a crossword, a couple who
spoke softly in a language I didn't know.

Nobody introduces themselves on a night train, and nobody needs to. There is a shared, unspoken
agreement about the light, the window and the time of the last conversation[^etiquette].

## The night

The best part of the trip is the hour after departure, when the city thins out into suburbs and
then into nothing at all. Station lights pass across the ceiling. Somewhere after midnight the
train stops for a long time in a place without a name, and you hear the carriages being coupled
to another engine, a gentle knock felt through the mattress.

> You don't so much travel through the night as get carried through it.

I slept badly, as everyone does the first time, and then very well.

## The morning

At six the attendant knocked with coffee and a roll. Outside, the landscape had changed completely:
mountains where there had been fields, wooden houses with steep roofs, snow still lying in the
shade. I had crossed two borders without noticing either. The flight would have been faster, on
paper. But the flight would have meant a taxi, a queue, a security check, a gate, a second queue,
a taxi again, and a day gone. The train took the night, which I was going to spend lying down
anyway[^cost].

## A few practical notes

Book a couchette rather than a seat; the difference in price is small and the difference in sleep
is not. Bring earplugs, a scarf for the draught, and slippers for the corridor. And bring more food
than you think: the dining car, if there is one, closes early, and the night is long.

[^etiquette]: Lights off by eleven, the window shut unless all agree, and the bottom bunk is the shared sofa until bedtime.
[^cost]: It also cost about the same as a flight and a hotel night together, which surprised me.
